# A Beginner‑Friendly Guide to Single‑Head vs Multi‑Head Attention (MHA) and KV‑Caching


Transformers have changed how we think about sequence modeling, and at the heart of these models is the **attention mechanism**. In this blog, we’ll walk step by step through:

- what `d_model` and `D` mean,  
- the difference between single‑head and multi‑head attention,  
- why the KV‑cache formula is \(2 \times L \times H \times D\),  
- and how Grouped‑Query Attention (GQA) and Multi‑Query Attention (MQA) build on this.

All of this is explained via **concrete examples** with small numbers so you can see exactly what happens inside the model.

***

## 1. What do `d_model` and `D` mean?

Before we talk about attention, let’s clarify two common symbols:

- **`d_model`** is the **embedding / hidden size** per token.  
- **`D`** (also called `d_head`) is the **dimension of each attention head**.

For example, assume:

- \(d_\text{model} = 512\): every token is represented as a 512‑dimensional vector.  
- Number of heads \(H = 4\).  

Then, the head dimension must satisfy:

\[
d_\text{model} = H \times D
\]

So:

\[
D = \frac{d_\text{model}}{H} = \frac{512}{4} = 128
\]

In short:

- **`d_model`** = the total width of the model per token.  
- **`D`** = the width of each “lane” when you split that total width into `H` parallel heads.  

This is important because queries, keys, and values are all computed in these dimensions.

***

## 2. Single‑head attention: one big lens

Let’s look at a specific setup:

- \(d_\text{model} = 512\)  
- \(H = 1\) head  
- \(D = 512\)  
- \(L = 101\) tokens  
- batch size \(B = 1\)

### Step 1: Input and embedding

- Input: 101 tokens.  
- Each token is mapped to a 512‑dim vector.  
- Shape:  
  \[
  X \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}
  \]

### Step 2: Project to Q, K, V

Three linear projections:

- \(W_Q, W_K, W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times 512}\)

Apply them:

\[
Q = X W_Q \quad\in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}
\]
\[
K = X W_K \quad\in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}
\]
\[
V = X W_V \quad\in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}
\]

Because there is only one head, we don’t need to reshape; everything stays in 512‑D.

### Step 3: Compute attention scores

Scaled dot‑product attention:

\[
\text{scores} = Q K^T \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 101}
\]

This is a **101 × 101** matrix of attention scores; each row tells one token “how much it attends to the other 101 tokens.”

Apply softmax over the last dimension:

\[
\text{attn} = \text{softmax}(\text{scores}) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 101}
\]

### Step 4: Weighted‑sum output

\[
\text{attn\_out} = \text{attn} \cdot V \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}
\]

Then, typically:

\[
Z = X + \text{attn\_out} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}
\]

followed by layer normalization (same shape).

***

## 3. Multi‑head attention (4 heads): many parallel lenses

Now keep \(d_\text{model} = 512\), \(L = 101\), \(B = 1\), but use:

- \(H = 4\) heads  
- \(D = 128\) (because \(4 \times 128 = 512\))

The overall **input** and **final output** dimensions stay the same:

\[
X \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512},\quad
Z \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}
\]

but the work inside is split differently.

### Step 1: Project Q, K, V

Same as before:

\[
Q, K, V \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}
\]

Now reshape along the last dimension to split into 4 heads of 128:

\[
Q^{\text{head}} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 4 \times 128}
\]

and similarly for \(K^{\text{head}}, V^{\text{head}}\).

### Step 2: Per‑head attention

For each head \(h = 1,\dots,4\):

- \(Q_h, K_h, V_h \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 128}\)  
- Compute scores:  
  \[
  \text{scores}_h = Q_h K_h^T \in \mathbb{R}^{101 \times 101}
  \]
- Softmax:  
  \[
  \text{attn}_h \in \mathbb{R}^{101 \times 101}
  \]
- Weighted‑sum:  
  \[
  O_h \in \mathbb{R}^{101 \times 128}
  \]

So you compute **4 separate 101 × 101 attention matrices** and get **4 outputs of 128‑D each**.

### Step 3: Concatenate heads and project

- Concatenate heads:  
  \[
  O_{\text{cat}} = [O_1, O_2, O_3, O_4] \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}
  \]
- Apply final projection \(W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times 512}\):  
  \[
  \text{attn\_out} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}
  \]

Residual + layer‑norm keeps the same shape:

\[
Z \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}
\]

Multi‑head attention thus **splits the 512‑D space into 4 specialized 128‑D heads**, then combines them back into the same 512‑D representation.

***

## 4. Why is the KV‑cache \(2 \times L \times H \times D\)?

During inference, Transformers reuse information from already‑generated tokens. The **key** (K) and **value** (V) vectors for past tokens are stored in the **KV‑cache** so they don’t need to be recomputed.

The KV‑cache for one layer, one batch, and one head stores:

- for each token (length \(L\))  
- for each head (\(H\))  
- for each head dimension (\(D\))  

Both **K** and **V** are stored, hence the factor of 2.

So:

\[
\text{KV‑cache elements} =
2 \times L \times H \times D
\]

### Intuition: Queries vs Keys/Values

- **Keys and Values** are “past state”: once computed for a token, they are stored so later tokens can attend to them.  
- **Queries** are “current”: computed on‑the‑fly for each new token and then discarded. They are **not cached**, so query size does not appear in the KV‑cache formula.  

### Concrete example

Take \(L = 101\), \(H = 1\), \(D = 512\):

\[
2 \times 101 \times 1 \times 512 = 103{,}040 \text{ elements}
\]

Now change to \(H = 4\), \(D = 128\):

\[
2 \times 101 \times 4 \times 128 = 103{,}040 \text{ elements}
\]

The **total KV‑cache size is the same**, but:

- single‑head: 1 big 512‑D head  
- 4‑head: 4 small 128‑D heads  

This is why multi‑head attention does not increase KV‑cache size; it just reorganizes the computation.

***

## 5. Single‑head vs multi‑head: side‑by‑side overview

Here is a compact comparison (same \(d_\text{model} = 512\), \(L = 101\), \(B = 1\)):

| Aspect | Single‑head (\(H=1\)) | 4‑head (\(H=4\)) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Input shape \(X\) | \(\mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}\) | \(\mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}\) |
| Q, K, V after projection | \(\mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}\) | \(\mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}\) |
| After head splitting | \(Q/K/V \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}\) (1 head) | \(Q^\text{head} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 4 \times 128}\) |
| Attention scores | 1 matrix \(\mathbb{R}^{101 \times 101}\) | 4 matrices \(\mathbb{R}^{101 \times 101}\) |
| Total attention scores | 10,201 | 40,804 |
| Output per head | 1 × \(\mathbb{R}^{101 \times 512}\) | 4 × \(\mathbb{R}^{101 \times 128}\) |
| After concatenation | Already \(\mathbb{R}^{101 \times 512}\) | \(\mathbb{R}^{101 \times 512}\) |
| Final attention output \(Z\) | \(\mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}\) | \(\mathbb{R}^{1 \times 101 \times 512}\) |
| KV‑cache elements | \(2 \times 101 \times 1 \times 512 = 103{,}040\) | \(2 \times 101 \times 4 \times 128 = 103{,}040\) |

**Key idea:** multi‑head attention keeps the same input and output shapes and the same total KV‑cache size; it simply distributes the computation into multiple smaller heads, which can learn different types of patterns in parallel.

***

## 6. How GQA and MQA build on this

Modern large models replace full Multi‑Head Attention (MHA) with **Grouped‑Query Attention (GQA)** or **Multi‑Query Attention (MQA)** to reduce memory and compute.

### MQA (Multi‑Query Attention)

- All queries share **one KV head** across all heads.  
- KV‑cache size: \(2 \times L \times 1 \times D\)  
- **Smallest KV‑cache and fastest**, but **weaker modeling capacity** because all heads see the same K/V.

### GQA (Grouped‑Query Attention)

- Split \(H\) query heads into \(G\) groups, each with its own KV head.  
- KV‑cache size: \(2 \times L \times G \times D\)  

If \(H = 32\), \(G = 8\), \(D = 128\) (typical large‑language‑model setup):

- MHA: \(2 \times L \times 32 \times 128\) → full cache  
- GQA: \(2 \times L \times 8 \times 128\) → ¼ of MHA  
- MQA: \(2 \times L \times 1 \times 128\) → ~1/32 of MHA  

**Trade‑off:**

- MQA: fastest, smallest memory, lowest quality.  
- GQA: good balance: MHA‑like quality with much smaller cache and faster inference.  

In practice, GQA‑8 is now the default in many 30B–70B models; MQA is used when latency or memory is more important than squeezing out the last bit of quality.

***

## 7. Wrapping up

We’ve walked through:

- how `d_model` and `D` relate to each other and to attention heads,  
- how single‑head attention works with big 512‑D vectors,  
- how multi‑head attention splits 512‑D into 4 × 128‑D heads but keeps the same input and output shapes,  
- why the KV‑cache formula is \(2 \times L \times H \times D\),  
- and how GQA and MQA compress the KV‑cache by sharing KV heads across query heads.

If you remember just a few things:

- `d_model = H × D`: heads divide the embedding space.  
- single‑head = one big 512‑D attention; multi‑head = multiple smaller heads in parallel.  
- KV‑cache stores K and V, not Q, which is why the formula is \(2 \times L \times H \times D\).  
- GQA and MQA reduce the number of KV heads to speed up inference and cut memory, with GQA sitting in the sweet spot between MHA and MQA.
